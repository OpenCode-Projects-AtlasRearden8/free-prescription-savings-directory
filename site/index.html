Manufacturer Savings Programs

Drug companies run their own savings programs: copay cards for people with commercial (workplace or marketplace) insurance, and separate patient assistance programs for people who are uninsured or on low income. One big rule to know up front: copay savings cards generally cannot be used with Medicare, Medicaid, TRICARE, or other government insurance. If you have government coverage, look at the company's patient assistance program or the Copay Accumulator note below.

Lilly Insulin Value Program (Eli Lilly)

Lilly's official savings program for its insulins (Humalog, Basaglar, Humulin, and more): download a savings card and pay no more than $35 a month per fill, whether you have commercial insurance or no insurance and pay cash.

Eligibility, in plain English

Open to US residents 18+ with a valid prescription. People enrolled in federal government insurance programs (Medicare, Medicaid, TRICARE) can't use the savings card, but federal law caps Medicare Part D insulin copays at $35 a month, so Medicare folks are covered a different way. The card expires at year end, so always grab the current one.

Cost

Free to download the card. Insulins max out at $35/month for eligible people.

HealthWell Foundation

A large national charity that gives grants covering copays, premiums, deductibles, and travel costs for people who can't afford out-of-pocket medicine costs. Funds open and close by disease area, so timing matters, though they're often generous when open.

National Discount Card Programs

These are free pharmacy discount cards and apps. You hand the card (or app code) to the pharmacy instead of using insurance, and the negotiated cash price is often lower than the sticker price or even a copay. They're free, legitimate, and you can use them even if you have insurance (though you usually can't combine a discount card WITH your insurance claim on the same fill).

GoodRx

The best-known prescription discount card. Search a drug name, see prices at nearby pharmacies, and show the coupon code at the counter or in the app. Also has a free savings card you can keep in your wallet.

Eligibility, in plain English

No sign-up required for most coupons; free for everyone including people with insurance. Not valid with insurance claims on the same purchase, and not accepted for government-program purchases like Medicare Part B drugs. A paid GoodRx Gold membership exists you can skip.

Cost

Free coupons and card. Optional paid tier (GoodRx Gold) you can skip.

Pharmacy Price Comparison Tools

Before you drive anywhere, spend five minutes comparing prices. The same generic drug can vary by $40 or more between pharmacies in the same town. These tools show you the range, and two of them can even ship the medicine to you.

GoodRx Price Comparison

Enter a drug (and dose) and see current estimated prices at pharmacies near you, plus national averages. Great for spotting when the pharmacy across the street is half the price of the one you usually use.

Eligibility, in plain English

Free, no account needed to search. Prices are estimates and change; the coupon shown at checkout is what you actually pay.

Free.

$4 Generics & Flat-Price Lists

Several big chains publish flat-price lists: a fixed short menu of common generics sold for $4 for a 30-day supply or $10 for 90 days, no card or membership needed. Ask the pharmacy for their current list or check the links. These programs have been around for years (Walmart started in 2006), but they do change, so always match your exact drug, dose, and quantity against the current list.

Walmart $4 / $10 Prescription Program

Walmart's famous flat-price program: a set list of common generics sold for $4 (30-day supply) or $10 (90-day supply) at Walmart and Sam's Club pharmacies, with some drugs in higher tiers (about $9-$24). Expect classics like lisinopril, metformin, and atorvastatin.

Eligibility, in plain English

Anyone can use it, no insurance or card needed, and you don't have to be a regular Walmart shopper. Not every generic is covered and pricing is 'starting at,' so match your exact drug, dose, and quantity against the current list. Ask the pharmacist to run your prescription against the program.

Cost

$4 or $10 per qualifying prescription; the program itself is free.

Cost

HRSA Find a Health Center

The federal government's official map of community health centers: clinics that treat you on a sliding fee scale based on income, whether or not you have insurance. Many also dispense low-cost or free prescriptions on site.

Eligibility, in plain English

Open to everyone, no insurance required, no immigration status requirement for the sliding scale at most centers. Fees are based on your income and family size, so low-income patients often pay little or nothing.

Cost

Free to search the map; visit costs are sliding-scale.

PAN Foundation (now part of Patient Advocate Foundation)

A charity that provides grants covering out-of-pocket medication copays (and often premiums and transport) for people who can't afford them. In 2026 it merged with Patient Advocate Foundation, and its funds now accept people on Medicare, Medicaid, or TRICARE. Disease funds open and close as money becomes available, so timing matters.

Good-to-know notes

no medical advice

This entire directory is information and navigation only. It is never medical advice. We do not diagnose, treat, prescribe, or recommend any medicine or treatment for anyone. If you have questions about your own care, your prescriber and your licensed pharmacist are the right places to start.

stacking rule

One discount at a time: you usually can't combine a manufacturer copay card, a pharmacy discount card, and insurance on the same fill. Ask the pharmacist which one is cheapest for this specific prescription.

medicare note

If you're on Medicare, the Medicare Prescription Payment Plan (spreading out-of-pocket drug costs into monthly payments) and the $2,000 annual Part D out-of-pocket cap (for 2025 onward) may matter to you; compared with the low prices here, ask your plan or State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP) for help.

pharmacist tip

The pharmacist (not the cashier) is the person to ask about the cheapest way to fill this prescription. Ask directly: 'What's the cash price with your discount card vs. my insurance?'
